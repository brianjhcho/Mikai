## Overview

Asymmetric information is the condition in which different actors in a market or social interaction possess different information relevant to the transaction — when one party (seller, employer, financial intermediary) knows more than the other (buyer, worker, investor), the resulting equilibrium generally differs from what would occur under complete information and is generally not Pareto-efficient. The First Welfare Theorem states that, under idealized conditions (complete markets, perfect information, perfect competition), a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient — no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off — and this result underpins the normative presumption that free markets are efficient and that intervention requires special justification. [[entities/joseph-stiglitz]] and collaborators (including George Akerlof and Michael Spence, with whom Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Prize, and Bruce Greenwald) showed that asymmetric information is not a marginal imperfection but a generic feature of real markets, and that even small informational imperfections destroy the efficiency properties of competitive equilibria. Greenwald & Stiglitz (1986) established that with asymmetric information or incomplete risk markets, competitive equilibria are *generically* Pareto-inefficient — the presumption of market efficiency inverts to a **presumption of market failure**, across essentially all real markets rather than selected cases. This result is particularly damaging to [[concepts/market-fundamentalism]] because it is derived using the same formal tools (optimization, equilibrium analysis, rational agents) that the fundamentalist position relies on: it is a refutation from within the paradigm.

## Notes

**Key mechanisms**

- **Adverse selection (hidden types).** When sellers know more about quality than buyers (or borrowers know more about creditworthiness than lenders), raising prices or interest rates drives out good-risk participants, leaving a pool of worse risks. The market may not clear through price adjustment; instead, **credit rationing** and quantity constraints emerge. The composition of transactions is distorted: low-quality goods drive out high-quality ones (George Akerlof's "lemons" model of the used-car market is the canonical illustration), high-risk borrowers crowd out low-risk ones, and markets can unravel entirely.
- **Moral hazard (hidden actions).** When one party to a contract cannot observe the other's behavior, the party whose actions are hidden has weakened incentives to exert effort or take precautions. Once insured or employed, agents can take unobservable actions that shift risk or reduce effort; optimal contracts are second-best at best. Insurance markets and labor contracts are the canonical examples.
- **Non-existence of equilibrium.** Under some information structures, no competitive equilibrium exists at all — the market collapses or requires non-standard institutional arrangements (screening, signaling, non-linear pricing) to function.
- **Rent extraction.** The informed party can structure contracts to extract surplus from the less-informed party. Financial intermediaries, executives, and employers all exploit informational advantages in ways consistent with rational behavior but that generate [[concepts/economic-rents]] rather than reflecting marginal productivity.

**What this undoes.** The Greenwald-Stiglitz result inverts the default in applied economics and policy:

- *Before:* Assume markets are efficient; justify each intervention as correcting a specific identifiable failure.
- *After:* Assume markets are informationally inefficient; institutions, regulations, and redistribution are necessary structural features of any well-functioning economy, not exceptional corrections.

This directly supports [[entities/joseph-stiglitz]]'s broader argument that institutions and distribution are not separable from efficiency — they are constitutive of what efficient economic organization means under realistic informational conditions.

**Relation to inequality.** Information asymmetries are a primary mechanism through which Stiglitz explains high incomes at the top that are not captured by [[concepts/just-deserts-income-theory]]. Financial sector rents pre-2008 are the paradigm case: sophisticated actors designed and sold complex products that exploited informational advantages over clients, extracting fees while transferring risk. The income generated reflected information advantages, not genuine value creation.

**Relationship to other critiques.** Stiglitz's attack complements [[concepts/keynesian-aggregate-demand|Keynes']] attack from a different direction. Keynes showed that macro self-correction fails at the aggregate demand level; Stiglitz showed that micro efficiency fails at the level of informational microfoundations. Together they comprehensively undermine the neoclassical synthesis — both the Keynesian macro layer and the neoclassical micro layer contain fundamental problems.

**Limitation.** Asymmetric information establishes a market failure, not a case for any particular intervention. Whether regulation, public provision, standardization, or disclosure requirements are the right corrective depends on specifics that information economics alone cannot determine. The Hayekian response — that government agencies face their own information problems and political capture — remains a real constraint on interventionist conclusions. (Note the tension with the "presumption of market failure" framing above: the general result inverts the burden of proof toward intervention, while this qualification holds that the result alone does not select which intervention is warranted.)
